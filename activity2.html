Variable analysis & visualization

A deeper statistical pass on the same satellite dataset — this time classifying every variable by type and scale, and building a dedicated plot for each one to see what the data actually looks like.

DATA + ANALYSIS DOCUMENT

Use the tabs at the bottom of the embed above to browse: Raw Data → Analysis (the full variable table) → Plot of Variable_1 through _8 (one chart per variable).

Variables at a glance
Country of Operator/Owner
CategoricalNominal
Bar chart — Plot of Variable_1
Why nominal: a country name is just a label — "USA" isn't more or less than "Japan," there's no built-in order or numeric distance between categories. Why a bar chart: with this many distinct countries, a bar chart lets every category get its own row and its own readable label, and sorting by length makes the frequency comparison immediate. A pie would have crowded and unreadable slices once you pass 5–6 categories.
Users
CategoricalNominal
Pie chart — Plot of Variable_2
Why nominal: "Commercial," "Government," "Military" are unordered labels describing who operates the satellite, not a measurable quantity. Why a pie chart: the point here is proportion of the whole fleet — how dominant Commercial ownership is compared to everything else — which a pie communicates more directly than a bar would. It's drawn as a donut with the counts moved into a side legend instead of on-slice labels, since several categories are just 1–2 satellites and would overlap if labelled directly on their sliver.
Purpose
CategoricalNominal
Bar chart — Plot of Variable_3
Why nominal: mission purpose (Communications, Earth Observation, Navigation…) is a label with no inherent ranking. Why a bar chart: back to bars here rather than a pie, because Purpose has more categories than Users and several of them are close in count — bars make small differences between categories far easier to compare than thin pie wedges would.
Perigee (km)
NumericalRatio, Continuous
Histogram — Plot of Variable_4
Why ratio & continuous: perigee is a measured distance with a true zero (0 km would mean no orbit at all) and it can take any value in between, not just fixed steps. Why a histogram: a bar or pie chart doesn't make sense once every satellite has a slightly different number — binning the values into ranges is what reveals the actual shape of the data, in this case a huge cluster in low Earth orbit and a thin tail of satellites way out near geostationary altitude.
Apogee (km)
NumericalRatio, Continuous
Histogram — Plot of Variable_5
Same scale as Perigee: a true-zero, continuously measured distance, so a histogram is the only chart type here that shows how the values are actually distributed rather than just listing them. What it shows: the shape looks almost identical to Perigee for most satellites since LEO orbits are nearly circular — the few visible outliers are the GEO satellites, where perigee and apogee are both around 35,780 km.
Inclination (degrees)
NumericalRatio, Continuous
Histogram — Plot of Variable_6
Why ratio & continuous: inclination is an angle measured from 0°, and it can fall anywhere along that continuous scale. Why a histogram: it exposes something a table of 99 numbers never would at a glance — several distinct clusters (near-equatorial GEO satellites around 0°, mid-inclination constellations near 50–55°, and a big polar/sun-synchronous cluster near 97–98°), each corresponding to a real orbital design choice.
Period (minutes)
NumericalRatio, Continuous
Histogram — Plot of Variable_7
Why ratio & continuous: time-to-complete-one-orbit has a true zero and varies continuously with altitude. Why a histogram: since period is directly tied to orbit altitude (Perigee/Apogee), it shows the same two-cluster pattern — most satellites circling every ~90–115 minutes in LEO, and the GEO group taking the full ~1,436 minutes (24 hours) to match Earth's rotation.
Launch Mass (kg.)
NumericalRatio, Continuous
Histogram — Plot of Variable_8
Why ratio & continuous: mass has a genuine zero point and can take any value along the scale, down to fractions of a kilogram for the smallest CubeSats. Why a histogram: it makes the extreme skew of this dataset obvious — a mountain of small, sub-300 kg communications and Earth-observation satellites, with a long right-hand tail from the handful of multi-tonne GEO communications and military satellites.

Every categorical variable used a bar or pie chart because those depend only on counts per label; every numerical variable used a histogram because those depend on binning a continuous range — matching chart type to scale of measurement, not just to what looked good, is the actual point of this activity. The full working (raw counts, bin edges, and per-variable notes) lives in the Analysis tab of the embedded sheet above.
